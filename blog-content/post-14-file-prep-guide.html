The Solution: Exact File Specs That Get Approved First Try

Here is the cheat sheet. Print this out. Tape it to your designer's monitor. Tattoo it on their forehead if you have to. These are the specs that get your files through production without a single hiccup.

Resolution

This is the number one reason files get rejected. And it is the easiest one to fix.

  • Full-size files (1:1 scale): 150 DPI minimum. 300 DPI is overkill for a vehicle wrap viewed from three feet away and will create massive files that are slow to upload and process. 150 DPI at full size is the sweet spot.
  • Half-size files (1:2 scale): 300 DPI minimum. The math works out the same -- you are getting the same effective resolution.
  • Quarter-size files (1:4 scale): 600 DPI minimum. Again, same effective output.

The common mistake: designing at 72 DPI because that is what your monitor displays. Your monitor is not your print output. 72 DPI on a 20-foot box truck looks like a pixelated mess from 1997.

Before: Designer creates artwork at 72 DPI in Photoshop because it "looks fine on screen." Prints come out blurry and soft, especially on text and logos. Customer is furious.

After: Same design rebuilt at 150 DPI full size. Text is razor sharp. Logos are crisp. Color gradients are smooth. Customer posts it on Instagram and tags you.

Color Mode

Your file MUST be in CMYK color mode. Not RGB. Not Pantone spot colors mixed with CMYK. Pure CMYK.

  • CMYK is how printers think. It is Cyan, Magenta, Yellow, and Key (black).
  • RGB is how screens think. If you send an RGB file, the colors will shift when converted to CMYK during printing. That electric blue your customer approved? It is going to come out looking muted and dull.
  • Color profile: Use SWOP (Specifications for Web Offset Publications) or GRACoL as your CMYK profile. When in doubt, go with US Web Coated (SWOP) v2.

Before: Designer builds the file in RGB because Photoshop defaults to it. The vibrant red in the design prints as a muddy brownish-red. The shop eats the reprint cost.

After: Designer converts to CMYK before starting the design (not after). Soft-proofs on screen with the CMYK preview enabled. What they see is what prints.

Bleed

Bleed is the extra material that extends beyond the edge of your design. It is there so that when the wrap is trimmed and installed, you do not see any unprinted white edges.

  • Standard bleed: 0.125 inches (1/8 inch) on all sides minimum.
  • Recommended bleed for vehicle wraps: 0.25 inches (1/4 inch) on all sides. This gives your installer more room to work with on edges and curves.
  • Panel overlaps: If your design spans multiple panels, include at least 0.5 inches of overlap between panels so the installer can create clean seams.

Before: File has zero bleed. Design runs right to the edge. When the installer wraps around a bumper edge, there is a white strip visible. Job has to be redone.

After: File has 0.25 inch bleed on all sides. Installer has room to tuck and wrap every edge cleanly. Result is flawless.

File Format

Not all file formats are created equal. Here is what to send and what to avoid.

Format Status Notes
Adobe Illustrator (.ai) Preferred Outline all fonts. Embed all images.
PDF (high-res, press quality) Preferred Export as PDF/X-1a or Press Quality. Embed fonts.
EPS Accepted Outline fonts, embed images.
TIFF (flattened) Accepted CMYK, 150 DPI min, LZW compression OK.
PSD Accepted with caveats Flatten layers. CMYK. Watch file size.
JPEG Last resort Maximum quality (12 in Photoshop). CMYK. Compression artifacts are real.
PNG Avoid RGB only. Not suitable for print production.

The golden rule: if you can send a vector file, send a vector file. Illustrator or PDF is always your best bet. Your text stays sharp, your logos stay clean, and the RIP software can process it faster.

The 5 Most Common File Prep Mistakes (And How to Fix Them)

Mistake 1: Fonts Not Outlined

You send a beautiful file with custom typography. The print shop opens it and every line of text is replaced with a default font because they do not have your typeface installed.

The fix: In Illustrator, select all text and go to Type > Create Outlines. In InDesign, export as PDF/X-1a which embeds fonts automatically. Do this as your LAST step before exporting so you can still edit text up until that point.

Mistake 2: Linked Images Instead of Embedded

Your file references images stored on your designer's desktop. You send the .ai file but not the linked images. The print file opens with big red X marks where the photos should be.

The fix: In Illustrator, go to Window > Links. Select all linked images. Click the menu icon and choose "Embed Image." Or better yet, package the file (File > Package) which collects everything into one folder.

Mistake 3: Wrong Dimensions

Your designer designs for a "standard truck" without actually measuring the specific vehicle. The wrap shows up and it is six inches short on the passenger side because that model year has a slightly different body style.

The fix: Measure the actual vehicle. Every single time. Use a vehicle template as a starting point but always verify dimensions against the real thing. You can find templates from most vehicle template providers, but always ground-truth them with a tape measure.

Pro tip: Use RestylePro AI to preview wraps on actual vehicle images before you go to print. It helps catch sizing and design issues before they become expensive problems.

Mistake 4: Transparent or White Backgrounds Where There Should Not Be

The design has a white logo on a dark background, but the logo layer has transparency issues. Or the background does not fully extend to the bleed area so there is a thin white border when printed.

The fix: Flatten your file before export. Make sure your background color extends all the way to the bleed edge. And double-check that any "white" elements in your design are actually colored white, not just empty space.

Mistake 5: Submitting at the Wrong Scale Without Noting It

Your file is built at quarter scale but you do not tell the printer. They print it at 100% and now you have a wrap for a toy car.

The fix: Always clearly label the scale in your file name (e.g., "Jones_Plumbing_Van_QUARTER-SCALE.ai"). Better yet, include a note on the artboard or in the order notes. And adjust your DPI accordingly -- remember, quarter scale means 600 DPI.

Your Pre-Flight Checklist: Use This Before Every Single Submission

I want you to copy this checklist. Print it out. Make your designer initial every item before they send a file to print. This is your insurance policy against wasted days and angry customers.

Vehicle Wrap File Pre-Flight Checklist

  • Resolution is 150 DPI or higher at full size (or equivalent at reduced scale)
  • Color mode is CMYK (not RGB)
  • Color profile is US Web Coated (SWOP) v2 or GRACoL
  • Bleed is 0.25 inches on all sides minimum
  • All fonts are outlined or embedded
  • All images are embedded (not linked)
  • File format is .ai, .pdf (press quality), .eps, or flattened .tif
  • Dimensions match the MEASURED vehicle (not just a template)
  • Scale is clearly labeled in the file name if not 1:1
  • Background extends fully to bleed edges -- no white gaps
  • All layers are flattened (for raster files)
  • File name includes: Client_Vehicle_Side/Panel_Scale
  • File size is under 500MB (compress TIFFs with LZW if needed)
  • Visual proof has been approved by the customer
  • Panel overlaps are 0.5 inches minimum where applicable

Every single item on that list takes less than 30 seconds to verify. The whole checklist takes maybe five minutes. Five minutes versus three days of delay. That is the easiest math you will ever do.

Pro Tips From Shops That Never Get Files Rejected

I talk to high-volume shops every week. The ones doing $30K, $40K, $50K a month. Here is what they all have in common when it comes to file prep.

They Use Templates as Starting Points, Not Finished Products

Templates get you in the ballpark. But every vehicle is slightly different. Model year changes, aftermarket parts, different trim levels -- they all affect the wrap dimensions. The best shops measure every vehicle and adjust the template before designing.

They Build a File Prep SOP

Standard Operating Procedure. Written down. Step by step. Every designer in the shop follows the same process every time. No shortcuts, no "I will remember to do it." If it is not in the SOP, it does not happen. Take the checklist above and turn it into your shop's SOP today.

They Soft-Proof Everything

Before exporting, they turn on CMYK soft-proofing in their design software. This shows them approximately how colors will look in print. It is not perfect, but it catches the biggest surprises -- like that neon green that is going to print as pea soup.

They Name Files Like Professionals

Not "final_v2_REAL_final.pdf." More like "SmithPlumbing_2024TransitConnect_DriverSide_FullScale_CMYK_150DPI.pdf." When the file name tells the whole story, there is no confusion at any point in the process.

Frequently Asked Questions

What resolution should my vehicle wrap files be?

For full-size (1:1 scale) files, use 150 DPI minimum. If you are working at half scale, use 300 DPI. At quarter scale, use 600 DPI. These all produce the same effective print resolution. Going above these numbers just creates larger files without visible improvement -- vehicle wraps are viewed from several feet away, not inches.

What file formats does WePrintWraps accept for vehicle wrap printing?

We prefer Adobe Illustrator (.ai) or press-quality PDF files. We also accept EPS, flattened TIFF, and PSD files. JPEG is accepted as a last resort at maximum quality settings. Always use CMYK color mode regardless of format. Make sure all fonts are outlined and all images are embedded, not linked. Visit our products page to place your order once your files are ready.

How much bleed should I add to a vehicle wrap file?

We recommend a minimum of 0.25 inches (1/4 inch) of bleed on all sides. For panels that will overlap during installation, include at least 0.5 inches of overlap. Bleed ensures there are no white edges visible after the wrap is trimmed and installed on the vehicle. This is one of the most common reasons files get rejected, so always double-check before submitting.
